What a tide table taught me about empty states

Featured post2 October 20267 min readDesign

Last autumn I spent three days on a research boat in the Koster fjord with the team from the Nordic Ocean Institute. They were testing Tidepool, the field app we had built for them, and I was there to watch where it broke. It broke somewhere I had not designed at all: the tide table.

On the Swedish west coast the tide is a matter of centimetres. Wind and air pressure move the water more than the moon does, so the biologists check the gauge readings before every dive. Our table pulled those readings from a sensor on the harbour wall. When the sensor went quiet, the table showed two words: “No data.”

Blank is a message too

Nobody on deck read “No data” as a neutral statement. One researcher took it to mean the app was broken and restarted it twice. Another took it to mean the water was calm, and planned a sample run around it. The empty state was the most-read screen of the day, and it said nothing true.

An empty state is the screen people see at the exact moment they most need help.

Here is what the sync log actually held for that Wednesday morning:

station,time,level_cm,status
torshamnen,02:50,+14,ok
torshamnen,03:10,     ,offline
torshamnen,15:40,     ,offline
torshamnen,16:00,+9,ok

That is not an absence of data. It says when the gap started, when it ended, and what the water was doing on either side. Every part of it is useful to someone standing on a deck.

What we shipped instead

The new empty state answers three questions in plain sentences: what is missing, why, and what to do meanwhile. It names the gauge and the hours it was offline. It shows the predicted tide in a lighter tone, labelled as a prediction. And it says when the next reading is due, so nobody restarts the app to check.

None of this needed new engineering. The information was already in the log; we had simply decided, without noticing, that an empty table was not worth writing. Now I start every project by listing the screens that will be empty on day one, and I write those first. They are usually the ones people remember.

About

I’m Maya Lindqvist, a product designer and writer based in Gothenburg. I spent five years at Kartverk Studio and Norrsken Analytics designing maps, dashboards and the forms behind them. Since 2024 I have worked independently for research institutes, transit agencies, a credit union and city governments. My work starts where the data is messiest: offline sync, records older than the staff who use them, live feeds that drop out. I prototype in code so clients can test with their own numbers in the first weeks, and I run workshops that put engineers, analysts and the people who use the tool every day in one room. I write about all of it here, and once a month in Field Notes. Tell me about your project.

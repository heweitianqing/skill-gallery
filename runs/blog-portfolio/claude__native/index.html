What a tide table taught me about empty states

By Maya Lindqvist2 October 20267 min read

On the second morning of fieldwork for Tidepool, somewhere off the coast near Lysekil, the ship’s mate handed me a folded paper tide table. It had no loading spinner. No “nothing here yet” message. No illustration of a sad, empty box. It simply listed the tides for the whole year, printed months before anyone needed them.

That little booklet changed how I think about empty states.

An empty state is a promise

Most software treats the moment before data arrives as an awkward gap, and fills it with an apology. But the biologists on that boat weren’t waiting for nothing. They were waiting for something specific: the next sample, the next sync, the next low tide. The tide table answered that question before anyone asked it.

So we stopped designing empty screens. In Tidepool, every view that could be empty now shows what is expected to arrive, and when. A sampling station with no readings shows its planned schedule. An upload queue with nothing in it shows the last successful sync and the next window when the vessel is likely to have signal.

An empty screen should tell you what will be here, not apologize that nothing is.

Expected data is still data

The catch is that expected has to be a real state in the data model, not a design flourish. Here is a simplified version of the station record we ended up with:

Once the expected state existed in the data, the interface could be honest without anyone writing cheerful filler copy. The content was already there; we just had to stop hiding it.

What changed

Two things surprised me. Questions to the support channel about “missing” readings fell by more than half in the first month, because people could see that nothing was missing, only not yet due. And the screens we used to call empty became some of the most visited in the app. Crews opened them in the harbour each morning to plan their day.

A tide table is a humble object. But it treats the future as content, and it is still the best empty state I have ever seen.

04 About

Hej, I’m Maya

Maya Lindqvist Independent product designer, Gothenburg

I’m a product designer and writer based in Gothenburg. For the past seven years I’ve designed software for people whose work depends on data: marine biologists logging samples at sea, credit union staff moving members between systems, dispatchers watching a whole city’s trams in real time.

I care most about the unglamorous parts, like forms, empty states, error messages and the moment a chart is either understood or misread. I prototype in code because real data rarely behaves the way a mockup expects. Today I take on a few projects a year, run workshops for teams, and write about what I learn along the way, usually on the tram.

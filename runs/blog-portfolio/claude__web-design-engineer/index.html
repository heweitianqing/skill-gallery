Featured post

What a tide table taught me about empty states

Maya Lindqvist2 October 20267 min read

For the first two weeks of the Tidepool project, I designed the wrong screen. I had mocked up a busy, satisfying sample log: species counts, coordinates, photos, a neat sync indicator. Then I went out on the Nordic Ocean Institute's survey boat and watched what the biologists actually looked at. It was the screen I had barely designed at all, the one that said nothing had been logged yet.

Out at a sampling station, nobody starts with data. They start by waiting: for the boat to settle, for the tide to turn, for the right depth. The empty log stayed open for twenty minutes at a time, and all it offered was a grey illustration and the words "No samples yet."

The tide table was already the answer

Taped to the wheelhouse wall was a printed tide table. Every researcher checked it before every drop. It had no data for this station yet, but it told them what to expect: when the water would be high, when it would be slack, and how long they had.

That's what a good empty state does. It isn't an apology for missing content. It is content: the most useful thing you can tell someone before the real work begins.

What we changed

We replaced the illustration with three things the crew already wanted: the next tide turn, the last synced sample from this station, and a checklist for the drop. All of it is cached on the device, so it works with no signal twelve nautical miles out. The data behind it is small enough to fit in a text message:

Within a week the empty screen had a new nickname on board: the briefing. People started opening it on purpose before they reached a station, to plan the drop.

Three questions I ask now

When I design an empty state now, I ask three questions. What does this person know before there is any data? What will they need in the next five minutes? And what would they otherwise have to look up somewhere else, on a wall, in a notebook, or by asking a colleague?

If the answer to the last one is "a tide table taped to the wall", that's your empty state. Design it as if it's the most-used screen in the app, because it might be.

03 · About

Hello, I'm Maya

I'm a product designer and writer based in Gothenburg. I make software for people whose days are shaped by data: researchers on boats, credit union staff, transit dispatchers, and the civil servants who publish the datasets everyone else relies on.

I started at Kartverk Studio drawing maps and wayfinding, then spent three years at Norrsken Analytics designing dashboards people actually opened. Since 2024 I've worked independently, usually as the only designer on a small team. I prototype in code, I spend the first week of a project watching people work, and I write about what I learn so the next project starts a little smarter. Most weekends you'll find me on a ferry to the archipelago with a sketchbook.

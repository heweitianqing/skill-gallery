What a tide table taught me about empty states

2 October 2026/7 min read/Design

On a boat outside Lysekil, a marine biologist showed me a tide table with a blank column. I assumed something had failed to load. She barely looked at it. “Nothing to record yet,” she said, and went back to checking her equipment. For her, that space was information. For me, it looked like a broken interface.

We were testing Tidepool, an app for recording observations at sea. I had spent weeks making the populated screens clear: species names, coordinates, timestamps, photographs. The screen before the first observation had received considerably less attention. It said, with impressive confidence, “No data.”

Empty is a condition, not an explanation

Back in the studio, I wrote down everything that could produce an apparently empty screen. Perhaps nobody had visited this station. Perhaps someone had visited but found nothing. Perhaps observations existed on another device, waiting for a connection. Those situations needed very different responses, yet our interface gave them the same two words.

“An empty state should explain what we know, not just what we can’t show.”

The tide table worked because its reader already understood the context. Our software needed to carry that context with it. A volunteer on their first trip should not have to ask a colleague whether a blank screen means a quiet morning or lost work.

We started with a tiny data contract, written together with the developers. Before discussing illustrations or button labels, we agreed that an empty result needed a reason:

{
  "observations": [],
  "state": "not_yet_surveyed",
  "last_synced": "2026-09-28T08:15:00Z"
}

That distinction changed the copy. “No observations yet” became “This station hasn’t been surveyed.” When a survey was complete, the message became “Survey complete. No species recorded.” Offline, we showed when the device last checked for updates. Each message made a claim we could actually support.

Leave people with a next step

We also stopped treating every empty state as an invitation to add something. An unvisited station offered “Start a survey.” A completed survey offered its field notes. A device waiting to sync explained that saved observations were safe. Sometimes the most useful action was simply to carry on.

I still think about that blank column. Good information design gives absence a shape people can understand. The next time a screen looks empty, I’ll ask what happened before it became empty, and what someone needs to know next. The answer is rarely just a better illustration.

Hello, I'm Maya.

A curious mind.
A practical pair of hands.

I'm a freelance product designer and writer based in Gothenburg, on Sweden's west coast. I work with teams turning complicated information into software that feels clear, useful, and a little more human.

My projects tend to begin with questions and a notebook: on a research boat, beside a support desk, or in a room full of people who know more about the problem than I do. I move between research, interaction design, and working prototypes, staying close to the people building and using the product.

Before going independent, I designed at Kartverk Studio and Norrsken Analytics. Away from my desk, you'll usually find me swimming in cold water, drawing on the tram, or making a very slow cup of coffee.
